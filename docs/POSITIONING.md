A data expert would call it **managed data operations**: you run the unglamorous middle of the data pipeline for departments that can't staff it themselves.

## The problem

Every performance department has the same chain:

**Devices and tests → [the middle] → reports → decisions**

Departments have bought the two ends. They have the devices that produce data, and they have skilled staff who make decisions. In the middle, someone has to gather the files, catch what's missing or wrong, match the same athlete across systems, keep definitions straight, and get a report out on time. That work is nobody's job.

Pro teams and the best-funded programs hire a data engineer or analyst for it. Everyone else hands it to a coach, a sport scientist, or a graduate assistant, on top of their real job. That causes three things:

- **Numbers nobody fully trusts**, because nobody checked them.
- **Reports that arrive late**, because the work waits until someone has time.
- **Expensive people doing clerical work**, when they could be coaching or doing sport science.

So the problem is not a lack of data or a lack of software. **It's that the middle has no owner.** The problem heading on the site already says this: "Performance staff have the data. Nobody owns the work between collection and decision."

## What you're selling

You're selling that owner: one specialist who runs the middle, with software to do the repetitive parts. A data expert would map the four verbs like this:

| Your word | What a data expert calls it |
|---|---|
| Organize | Intake and record-keeping: every file kept, and what's missing logged |
| Check | Data quality |
| Connect | Matching records: one athlete, many systems |
| Deliver | Reporting, with documentation of what each number means |

## What it isn't

It's defined just as much by what it's not:

- **Not analytics or prediction.** You don't tell them what the data means for an athlete.
- **Not a software product.** They aren't buying a tool to learn and run.
- **Not consulting.** You don't advise and leave. You do the work every week.

A data expert would say that's a sensible place to stand. The middle is where most data efforts fail, and it's the part that software alone hasn't solved, because it needs a person's judgment and follow-through.

## One more fact (Jake, 2026-11-13)

Ignition APG works across the department's whole tech stack. A department may have an athlete management system, and the specialist works across it with everything else. Ignition APG is not an alternative to an AMS.
